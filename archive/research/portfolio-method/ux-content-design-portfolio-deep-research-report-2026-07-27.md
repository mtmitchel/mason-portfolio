## What hiring evidence actually rewards

**Documented guidance:** hiring signals consistently point away from “nice writing samples” and toward **product judgment**. Andy Welfle, then a content strategy lead and hiring manager at Adobe, framed portfolios as a common unknown for candidates and wrote from direct hiring responsibility. UX Content Collective’s portfolio discussion shows that many companies ask for a portfolio at application time and often run a dedicated portfolio-review interview where candidates must explain decisions, research, and outcomes. Jody Allard, drawing on hundreds of interview loops, says writing quality is only the baseline; what stands out is how a candidate thinks, collaborates, measures, prioritizes, and influences product direction. citeturn38view0turn38view2turn37view1

Current job descriptions reinforce that point. Recent postings ask for portfolios that show **systems thinking**, **measurable impact**, **problem solving**, and increasingly **AI or dynamic-content capability**. Monks asks for AI-specific design thinking, prompt engineering, training data, evaluation frameworks, and end-to-end conversational design. Carrot asks for systems thinking, exceptional UX writing, measurable product impact, and LLM evaluation practice. Peloton asks for a portfolio that shows how a candidate thinks and solves problems in AI-driven interfaces. Point asks for strong writing across multiple formats, research-informed writing, legal and compliance collaboration, and evidence that copy performance is monitored and iterated. Scaleway emphasizes simplifying complex technical concepts, localization quality, and the ability to work with Figma, Git, and Markdown. citeturn39view0turn39view3turn39view4turn39view5turn10search2

That directly answers the question of what recruiters and hiring managers “actually look for”: not just microcopy skill, but evidence that the candidate can function as a **product teammate**. Technical Writer HQ’s recent review says the portfolio is proof that you can solve interface problems with words, explain your decisions, and collaborate without drama. Van Schneider’s guide, based on discussions with UX people and job descriptions, similarly says hiring leaders are looking for experience on a design team, familiarity with design systems, and understanding of the end-to-end UX process. citeturn40view1turn40view0

**Documented guidance about the first 30 seconds:** portfolios are screened quickly, so speed matters. Angela Gorden says portfolios should be scannable because she makes up her mind fast, and wordy or dense portfolios make the reviewer work too hard. Yvonne Gando warns against spending too much time talking about yourself instead of the work and the problems solved. Jody Allard says she would rather see one tightly presented example than several rushed ones. In practice, the strongest homepages in this sample answer three questions immediately: **Who are you? What kind of content/design problems do you solve? Where do I click for proof?** citeturn41search17turn38view1turn37view1turn12view0turn23view3turn28view2

**My inference:** the public portfolios that would most likely survive an initial recruiter skim are the ones that reduce uncertainty fastest. They do that by making role, scope, and project relevance obvious within one screen; by surfacing work early; and by using project titles that imply stakes or outcomes rather than generic labels. Anne Dougherty’s homepage presents “Role / The work / Impact” on the front page. Nicole Alexandra Michaelis and Andrew Webb put large, specific work narratives near the top. Jeff Shibasaki splits writing and design immediately. Lisa Collins starts with a crisp value proposition and visible project stack. citeturn23view3turn28view2turn31search1turn23view2turn32view0

## Patterns shared by effective portfolios

The ranked list below is **my inference**, but it is grounded in the documented guidance above and in repeated patterns across the portfolio sample.

1. **Immediate clarity beats cleverness.** Strong portfolios make the role, specialty, and work entry points obvious above the fold. Andrew Schmidt, Lisa Collins, Anne Dougherty, Jeff Shibasaki, and Nicole Alexandra Michaelis all make it easy to understand what they do and where to go next. citeturn12view0turn32view0turn23view3turn23view2turn28view2

2. **Case studies feel important when they establish stakes early.** The best case studies do not begin with generic process boilerplate. They begin with a business or user tension: replacing Human onboarding at Wolt, launching a global subscription change at Pret, reducing confusing governance workflows, or designing a WHO cessation agent during COVID. Those openings make the work feel consequential rather than routine. citeturn28view2turn26search8turn23view3turn30view1

3. **“Final state first, process second” is more effective than chronological process dumps.** Voiceflow’s Elaine Anzaldo explicitly recommends showing the final outcome first and then walking through process. UXfolio says before-and-after screenshots are especially strong. Strong public portfolios in this sample follow that pattern with explicit final-state screenshots or direct statements of shipped outcomes. citeturn36view5turn14view0turn28view2turn23view3

4. **The strongest portfolios explain judgment, not just activity.** Jody Allard says she wants to understand the candidate’s questions, collaboration, use of research and data, and prioritization. The best portfolios show conscious tradeoffs: Sarah Ann explains how research changed hierarchy and messaging; Kento Morita explains how he linearized non-linear conversation design to fit legal and medical review; Nicole Michaelis explains how metrics and principles were defined and used. citeturn37view1turn26search5turn30view1turn28view2

5. **Outcomes matter disproportionately.** Carrot explicitly asks for measurable impact. Point asks candidates to monitor copy performance and iterate. Stronger portfolios use outcome framing even when the evidence is qualitative: Anne Dougherty foregrounds attendance growth and reduced support burden; Nicole Michaelis includes adoption and compliance improvements; Andrew Webb names revenue effects and operational stakes; Oz Chen cites launches, traffic, and capital-raising relevance. citeturn39view3turn39view5turn23view3turn28view2turn31search1turn23view0

6. **Systems thinking is a seniority signal.** Current jobs ask for frameworks, standards, design-system participation, localization, governance, and AI-content systems. That same pattern appears in the strongest portfolios: Sarah R. Kessler foregrounds AI design, onboarding emails, product naming, and voice and tone; Adina Cretu leads with language systems and AI strategy; Anne Dougherty shows design-system governance; Nicole Michaelis shows design-system and localization scale; Clem Auyeung foregrounds content engineering and IA. citeturn39view0turn39view3turn39view4turn28view1turn25view0turn23view3turn28view2turn27view1

7. **Good portfolios use visuals to support a writing argument, not to cosplay as visual design portfolios.** CareerFoundry notes that UX writing portfolios should spotlight written solutions rather than pretending to be standard UX design portfolios full of wireframes and journey maps. The strongest examples still use visuals heavily, but the visuals are in service of explaining language decisions: annotated screenshots, before/after comparisons, system artifacts, taxonomy examples, flow summaries, or demo videos for voice and chat. citeturn38view3turn14view0turn36view5turn26search11turn30view1

8. **Personality helps when it clarifies fit, not when it buries the work.** Several strong portfolios have a distinct voice: Andrew Schmidt, Darci Groves, Tyler Womack, and Suzanne Richards, for example. The common pattern is that personality is compressed into a short hook or tonal frame, while the work remains the focal point. That matches Yvonne Gando’s warning not to spend too much time on yourself instead of the work. citeturn12view0turn27view0turn27view2turn25view1turn38view1

9. **Three to five strong projects is usually enough; curation is more persuasive than volume.** Jody Allard explicitly says one strong example is better than four rushed ones. In the portfolio sample, many of the strongest portfolios foreground roughly three to six cases or project groups rather than exhaustive archives: Lisa Collins, Anne Dougherty, Sarah R. Kessler, Adina Cretu, Andrew Webb, and Nicole Alexandra Michaelis all fit that pattern. citeturn37view1turn32view0turn23view3turn28view1turn25view0turn31search1turn28view2


## Recurring failure modes

This ranked list is also **my inference**, supported by the documented guidance and by weaker patterns in the public sample.

1. **Generic case-study framing.** Titles like “Project X redesign” or “UX writing work” are too weak unless the subtitle immediately explains the stake, audience, or change. The strongest examples almost always attach a reason the work mattered. citeturn23view3turn28view2turn26search8turn31search2

2. **Dense pages with weak hierarchy.** Angela Gorden’s scannability warning is borne out by weaker sites that front-load heavy autobiography, put work behind too many clicks, or rely on blog chronology. Joel Edelman’s current front page, for example, is personable but noisier than a dedicated work-first homepage. Jas Deogan and Jef Blocker both warn that less is more and that readable hierarchy matters. citeturn41search17turn27view3turn38view4

3. **Process theater.** Hiring guidance consistently values the process that changed the decision, not folders full of methods. Jody Allard wants evidence of thinking; UXfolio warns against naming methods without explaining findings; Voiceflow recommends simple, familiar case-study structure rather than reinvention. citeturn37view1turn14view0turn36view5

4. **No visible impact.** A portfolio can still work without hard metrics, but it becomes weaker when it shows only polished screens and never says what changed for users, the business, or the team. Current postings are unusually explicit about wanting measurable impact, systems thinking, and outcomes. citeturn39view3turn39view5turn40view1

5. **Over-indexing on UI polish instead of language decisions.** CareerFoundry is right that content design portfolios are not standard UX design portfolios in disguise. When the page spends more time proving it can be a slick website than proving it can solve an information problem, it often reads junior or generic. citeturn38view3turn38view4

6. **Vague ownership.** When the portfolio hides the candidate’s role, collaborators, or constraints, reviewers are left guessing whether the candidate made the strategic call or merely polished copy at the end. The best portfolios make team composition and role highly legible. citeturn36view5turn28view2turn23view3turn31search2

7. **Too much “about me” before the proof.** Yvonne Gando explicitly warns about talking too much about yourself. The best sites keep personal framing short and route the reader to work fast. citeturn38view1turn12view0turn23view2

8. **Portfolio-as-blog instead of portfolio-as-proof.** Blog posts and thought leadership can help, but not if they displace project evidence. Several weaker examples in the sample drift toward personal-site behavior rather than a recruiter-ready proof set. citeturn27view3turn30view3

9. **Anonymous corporate tone.** Some portfolios read like internal status memos. Stronger ones are concise and professional, but still show a point of view. Suzanne Richards’ “Can banking content be a joy to read?” and Anne Dougherty’s “Content is the user experience” are stronger hooks than generic buzzword strings. citeturn25view1turn23view3

10. **Too much gating.** Password protection is acceptable for sensitive case studies, but hiding everything raises friction too early. UXfolio explicitly recommends case-study-level protection rather than portfolio-wide protection. citeturn14view0

## Recommended structures and visual storytelling playbook

**Recruiter-facing definition of excellence — documented guidance synthesized into one line:** an excellent UX writing or content design portfolio proves that the candidate can **solve product problems with language and structure, make decisions under real constraints, collaborate as a product teammate, and demonstrate outcomes or credible evidence of impact**. That definition is directly aligned with hiring-manager advice and current job ads. citeturn37view1turn38view2turn39view0turn39view3turn39view4turn39view5turn40view1

**Recommended homepage structure — my inference from the evidence:**

1. **Hero:** name, role, one-sentence value proposition, and one-sentence specialty. Keep it brutally clear. Andrew Schmidt, Lisa Collins, Jeff Shibasaki, and Nicole Michaelis handle this well in different styles. citeturn12view0turn32view0turn23view2turn28view2
2. **Featured projects immediately after the hero:** three to five project cards or grouped work samples. Lead with specific titles that imply stakes, not generic labels. Anne Dougherty, Sarah R. Kessler, Andrew Webb, and Suzanne Richards do this effectively. citeturn23view3turn28view1turn31search1turn25view1
3. **Credibility strip or compressed context:** selected companies, current role, short résumé link, or a small testimonial block. citeturn28view1turn28view2turn23view3
4. **Short “about/how I work” block:** enough to show fit and perspective, not a full memoir. citeturn38view1turn27view0turn27view2
5. **Visible contact path:** contact link, LinkedIn, email, or calendar. Do not make the reviewer hunt for it. citeturn38view1turn32view0turn30view3

**Recommended case-study structure — documented guidance plus synthesis:**

1. **Opening hook:** one paragraph or visual that states the product, audience, stake, your role, and why the problem mattered. Voiceflow recommends a quick summary with project length, team, methods, and tools; Nicole Michaelis and Anne Dougherty do this well. citeturn36view5turn28view2turn23view3
2. **Show the final or improved state early:** final screenshots, demo video, or a concise “what changed” panel. Elaine Anzaldo recommends this explicitly, and UXfolio says before-and-after is especially strong. citeturn36view5turn14view0
3. **Explain the problem and constraints:** what users struggled with, what the business needed, what was hard, and what constraints shaped the work. citeturn30view1turn26search8turn31search2
4. **Show judgment:** options considered, tradeoffs, pushback, prioritization, and why the winning direction won. That is what makes a case study feel senior. citeturn37view1turn28view2turn26search5
5. **Include only the process that changed the work:** research findings, iteration rounds, usability evidence, or alignment artifacts that materially influenced the content. Not every workshop photo needs to survive. citeturn14view0turn37view1turn36view5
6. **Close with outcomes:** hard metrics if you have them, strong qualitative evidence if you do not, plus what you learned. citeturn23view3turn28view2turn39view5turn39view3

**How much process is useful before it becomes tedious?** Process is useful until it stops answering “why did you make that call?” and starts becoming an unedited archive. UXfolio warns against listing research methods without explaining findings. Jody Allard wants to understand questions, collaboration, research, measurement, and prioritization, not to see every artifact ever produced. The best case studies in this sample are selective: Sarah Ann uses only the research and iteration needed to explain hierarchy choices; Kento Morita uses only the workflow change needed to explain faster legal/medical clearance; Nicole Michaelis highlights principles, user testing, and metrics because those changed the work. citeturn14view0turn37view1turn26search5turn30view1turn28view2

**Visual storytelling playbook for content designers — my inference, source-backed:**

1. **Use annotated before/after screenshots** when the change is local to language or hierarchy. UXfolio and Yvonne Gando both emphasize before/after in context. citeturn14view0turn38view1
2. **Show the writing in its habitat.** Screenshots beat links because they preserve context and prevent ambiguity about what you wrote. citeturn14view0
3. **Use one annotated flow map, not five generic diagrams.** Sarah Ann’s work shows how a small number of workshop and research artifacts can make a case study clearer without burying it. citeturn26search5turn26search10
4. **For systems work, show the artifact that scales.** Good examples include tone frameworks, naming systems, design-system components, IA changes, glossary or localization resources, and governance assets. Anne Dougherty, Nicole Michaelis, Sarah R. Kessler, Adina Cretu, and Clem Auyeung all benefit from this. citeturn23view3turn28view2turn28view1turn25view0turn27view1
5. **For conversation design, use demo video, transcript, or prototype.** Elaine Anzaldo says video or prototype is important because canvas views alone do not communicate the actual experience. Kento Morita and Chad Connally’s work supports that. citeturn36view5turn30view1turn30view2
6. **Use editorial pacing rather than visual-density tricks.** Strong content-design portfolios read more like good feature stories than like giant slide dumps: short sections, clear headings, one visual point per section, and a steady alternation between claim and proof. citeturn23view3turn28view2turn26search8
7. **Borrow structure from strong editorial websites, not necessarily from other UX portfolios.** Fredrika.dev is a useful reference here: not because it is a UX writing portfolio, but because it demonstrates a lean homepage, strong hierarchy, and long-form docs-style storytelling that content designers can adapt. citeturn8search0turn8search1


**Case-study evaluation rubric — my inference from the documented priorities above:**

| Criterion | Weight | What excellent looks like |
|---|---:|---|
| Problem framing and stakes | 20 | The opening makes the user/business tension obvious and worth caring about |
| Judgment and rationale | 20 | The candidate shows tradeoffs, decision points, prioritization, and why one direction won |
| Evidence and outcomes | 20 | Metrics, qualitative evidence, adoption signals, or credible proxy measures show what changed |
| Collaboration and constraints | 15 | Role, teammates, legal/localization/technical constraints, and scope boundaries are explicit |
| Writing craft in context | 15 | Screenshots or demos show clarity, tone, hierarchy, and appropriateness in the actual product context |
| Systems thinking | 5 | The case hints at pattern-making, governance, scale, localization, terminology, or design-system thinking |
| Scannability and storytelling | 5 | The page is easy to skim and keeps attention without fluff |

This weighting reflects what documented sources emphasize most: judgment, process, outcomes, collaboration, and the ability to think like a product teammate rather than merely a copywriter. citeturn37view1turn38view2turn39view0turn39view3turn39view4turn39view5turn40view1

## Portfolio options and final recommendation

**Five differentiated portfolio directions — my inference:**

| Direction | Best for | Advantages | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Editorial product storyteller | Mid-level content designers applying broadly | Strong narrative, easy recruiter skim, good for user-flow case studies | Can look light on systems/ops if not balanced |
| Systems-and-scale specialist | Senior/staff roles, platform, design systems, regulated products | Signals seniority, leverage, governance, localization, standards | Can feel abstract if there are too few product moments or screens |
| Domain specialist | Fintech, health, enterprise SaaS, policy-heavy products | Makes complexity legible and shows transfer value fast | Can narrow appeal if every case looks identical |
| AI and conversation portfolio | AI UX, assistants, dynamic UI, conversation design roles | Strong fit with current postings; demo-friendly; differentiates quickly | Can look niche or speculative if it lacks shipped product evidence |
| Hybrid strategic generalist | Candidates targeting content design roles with varied titles | Matches real hiring market where titles blur; shows flexibility across product, lifecycle, and systems | Easy to become unfocused unless carefully curated |
