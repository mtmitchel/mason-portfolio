## Executive summary

The export contains a substantial, connected body of work around the launch and monetization of DeepL Write Pro. The clearest activity runs from February through June 2024, with later terminology and conversion work in October and November 2024.

The work appears to have had three main layers:

1. **Launch and product positioning:** explain DeepL Write Pro as a professional writing assistant, initially with a strong B2B orientation and later with a more explicitly enterprise-ready product page.
2. **Free-to-paid conversion:** improve blocker messages, trial prompts, character-limit messages, feature-benefit lists, CTAs, and the route from the free version to a 30-day Write Pro trial.
3. **Terminology governance:** define the full, shortened, and space-constrained names for DeepL Write plans, Translator plans, bundles, and the free version so product and website teams could ship consistent UI.

Repeated user feedback shows that the desired writing was restrained and product-specific: clear, concise, explicit, easy to localize, professionally conversational, and less promotional than many of the generated drafts.

## Reconstructed project goals

Based on the repeated prompts and attached materials, the likely goals were to:

- Introduce and explain DeepL Write Pro.
- Show the practical difference between the free version and Write Pro.
- Convert users when they reached free character or usage limits.
- Promote a free 30-day trial without hiding the relevant limitation or overloading the interface.
- Present concrete benefits rather than vague claims: unlimited text improvements, alternatives, styles and tones, integrations, security, and professional communication.
- Support both individual product use and B2B/enterprise purchasing contexts.
- Make the website and product UI use consistent names for products, plans, bundles, and subscriptions.
- Create copy that could be localized safely, especially in short UI surfaces such as buttons, blockers, badges, and modals.
- Later, make the DeepL Write web presence more enterprise-ready and more product-led.

## Terminology rules recovered from the paid-plans launch document

The following rules are historical but directly supported by the extracted launch document.

### DeepL Write plans

| Full term | Shortened term | Very short term | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepL Write Pro | Write Pro | Pro, only when Write and the meaning are already clear | A paid plan for DeepL Write |
| DeepL Write for Business | Write for Business | Business | A paid plan for DeepL Write |

### Translator and Write bundles

The extracted source lists these historical bundle forms:

- DeepL Pro Starter + Write Pro
- DeepL Pro Advanced + Write Pro
- DeepL Pro Ultimate + Write Pro

The source explicitly says that a bundle is not itself a plan. “Bundle” is a descriptive noun for plans sold together and can also be used as a verb.

### “Pro” and “DeepL Pro”

- “Pro” by itself was **not** an acceptable umbrella term for all paid Translator and Write plans.
- “DeepL Pro” did not identify a single plan in the source document. At that time it referred collectively to paid Translator plans.
- “Pro” could appear as the shortest form of DeepL Write Pro only when the Write context and meaning were already unmistakable.
- Later naming conversations show active debate and evolving conventions, so these historical names should not be assumed to match current DeepL nomenclature.

### Plan, subscription, free version, and bundle

- A plan is the product offering or entitlement being discussed.
- The launch source says not to use “subscription” as a synonym for “plan.”
- “Free version” is not a plan; it describes the product access available when a user is not on a paid plan.
- A bundle is not a plan; it combines two or more plans.
- Later copy-review instructions sharpened the distinction by using “plan” in marketing or selection contexts and “subscription” for the post-purchase relationship where appropriate.

## Content and voice rules that shaped the launch

The embedded DeepL guides provide a clear editorial standard.

### Brand voice

The four recurring voice characteristics are:

- Helpful
- Empathetic
- Self-assured
- Professional

The guidance describes professional as polite and friendly, not cold or overly excited. It asks writers to put technology at the service of people, explain complex technology simply, and focus on the benefit to the user.

### Writing principles

The recovered guide lists these principles:

- Start with the customer.
- Be a leader in AI.
- Get specific about solutions.
- Do not just claim superiority; prove it.
- Write with clarity.

### Mechanics and UI style

- Use active voice where possible.
- Use simple, clear American English for native and non-native readers.
- Use sentence case for headings, subheadings, CTAs, and UI copy.
- Capitalize proper nouns, acronyms, and official product and plan names; use lowercase otherwise.
- Use the Oxford comma.
- Use contractions when they make copy shorter and more human, except in formal or high-risk contexts where a negative contraction might be misread.
- Avoid ampersands unless space makes them necessary.
- Use exclamation points sparingly.
- Avoid periods in short interface elements such as headings, buttons, labels, hover text, and single-fragment bullets.
- Give users an actionable next step in errors and blockers.

### Additional constraints established through iteration

Mason's feedback adds practical launch-specific rules:

- Do not sound “markety,” grandiose, or clichéd.
- Prefer explicit benefits over metaphors and vague aspiration.
- Avoid jargon, buzzwords, slang, idioms, and colloquial language.
- Do not use title case.
- Keep button labels especially simple.
- Maintain grammatical parallelism across feature lists.
- Avoid repeating the same word in close proximity.
- Do not overpromise with words such as “instantly,” “perfect,” or “seamless” unless the product can support the claim.
- Keep localization in mind from the beginning.
- Name the correct product and plan instead of using generic “Pro.”
- State limitations accurately, including the amount of text processed when that context helps the user.
- Make the headline, supporting text, and CTA perform distinct jobs.

## Reconstructed UX journey

The export supports this approximate journey:

1. A user encounters DeepL Write through the product page, an integration, or the free product.
2. The product page explains clear writing, alternatives, styles and tones, integrations, unlimited use, and security.
3. The free product allows a bounded amount of text or usage.
4. At a limit, the UI explains what happened—for example, only 2,000 of 4,182 characters were improved.
5. The UI presents Write Pro as the way to continue with unlimited characters or text improvements.
6. The user can learn more, dismiss the prompt, or start a free 30-day trial.
7. After activation, the broader paid experience may introduce multiple DeepL capabilities, which increases the importance of distinguishing DeepL Write Pro from Translator plans and bundles.
8. Enterprise prospects encounter a more product-led page emphasizing professional communication, administration, integrations, security, and privacy.
