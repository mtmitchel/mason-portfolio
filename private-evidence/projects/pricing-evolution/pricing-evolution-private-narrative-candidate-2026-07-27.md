**Private status:** Working draft for Mason's review only. Mason chooses the
title, headings, copy, format, and images. Site implementation, asset export,

**Candidate title:** Making the more expensive plan explain itself

---

DeepL's Translator pricing page put four plans side by side and asked people to pick one. Two of them said almost the same thing: Advanced cost €24.99 per user per month, Ultimate cost €49.99, and both cards listed the same eight benefits in the same order. I restructured the cards with the product team so each plan stated only what it added to the one below it. When a later layout came back without the line that named what each plan inherited, I asked for it back. The cumulative structure shipped, and DeepL's pricing page still uses it.

## €49.99 repeated everything €24.99 already said

The paid cards ran as checklists. Starter carried six lines; Advanced and Ultimate carried eight each. Advanced's eight and Ultimate's eight were the same eight, in the same order, and six of them matched word for word — data security, unlimited text translation, shared glossaries, team administration, single sign-on, CAT tool integration.

Two lines carried the entire difference. Advanced said "20 editable file translations per user/month in total" and "Upload files up to 20 MB in size." Ultimate said "100 editable file translations per user/month in total" and "Upload files up to 30 MB in size." The price doubled; two numbers moved.

That put the work on the reader. Someone deciding whether to spend another €25 per user per month had to read two columns in parallel and hunt for the numbers that changed. The card charged for the upgrade without naming it.

The lines themselves slowed the comparison down. Most ran to two or three rows inside a narrow column, so the eye had to track wrapped text sideways to line the benefits up. Enterprise, at the far right, did not use the same vocabulary at all — it offered "Market-leading translation accuracy" and "Globally secure and compliant" while its neighbours counted files and megabytes. At its most expensive step, the grid stopped answering the same question.

## I made each card start where the one before it stopped

The rule was easy to state and awkward to apply: name a benefit on the plan that introduces it, and let every plan above it say in one line what it carries forward. Team administration arrives with Starter, so it belongs on Starter and nowhere above. Single sign-on and CAT tool integration arrive with Advanced, so they appear once, on Advanced. Ultimate raises two limits, so Ultimate lists two limits.

Working on the grid with the product team, I set two goals for the benefit wording. Each line should fit a single row, and the same benefit should read the same way in every column. "Upload files up to 20 MB in size" became "20 MB maximum file size." "2,000 glossaries with 5,000 entries each, shared with your team" became "2,000 shared glossaries (5,000 entries each)." The facts stayed. The sentence shapes stopped competing with the comparison the columns were there to make.

## Six lines came off Ultimate, and one line had to go back

When the work moved onto a new layout, the repeats were gone but the inheritance heading had not survived the move. Cutting without that heading would have been worse than not cutting at all. Strip six lines off Ultimate and say nothing about what it keeps, and DeepL's most expensive paid plan shows two benefits while the free plan shows four. The shortest list reads as the smallest offer.

So I asked for the line back. In the revised state, each paid card opens with what it inherits — "All Free features, plus:", "All Starter features, plus:", "All Advanced features, plus:" — and then lists only its own additions. Ultimate's whole card is that heading and two lines: "100 file translations/month" and "30 MB maximum file size." One sentence does the work of the six lines it replaced, and the two that justify the price are the only two left standing.

The pattern also gave the two ends of the grid somewhere to stand. Enterprise picked up "All Ultimate features, plus:" and four additions, so the most expensive column finally answered the same question as the rest. A Free tier anchored the bottom, which gave "All Free features, plus:" something to point at. A fifth column fit into the same grid: Advanced dropped from eight lines to five under its heading, and Ultimate from eight to two.

## Two years on, the page still says "Everything in Individual, plus:"

The cumulative structure shipped. DeepL later renamed the paid Translator plans — Starter, Advanced and Ultimate became Individual, Team and Business — and kept the pattern. On the public pricing page today, Team opens with "Everything in Individual, plus:" and Business opens with "Everything in Team, plus:", each followed by its own additions. The names changed. The structure held.

What people see on that page is a deletion: six repeated lines gone from the most expensive card. What made the deletion work is the line I put back above it. A plan card can drop what the reader already has, but only while it is still saying what the reader is getting.
