# About this style guide {#about-this-style-guide .unnumbered}

> At DeepL, we are on a mission to overcome language barriers and bring
> cultures closer together. As a language technology company, we aim to
> display clear and seamless copy wherever users and customers interact
> with our product and business. Our UI is one of our fmost powerful
> levers to show that we care about language. The purpose of this style
> guide is to define DeepL's voice and tone and offer a set of language
> rules, in order to:
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} achieve consistency across all
> company copy regardless of the copywriter
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} save time for copywriting and
> translating tasks
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} make sure that our copy and
> translations have the quality we expect to provide the best user
> experience
>
> The needs and the contents of our style guide will evolve so it will
> be revisited periodically to include new elements as new questions
> come up.

# Voice and tone {#voice-and-tone .unnumbered}

> **Voice**
>
> Picture a person that you know and think of their voice. Same here:
> DeepL's voice is consistent across all copy, even if the copy is of
> different nature. The voice remains the same across all languages: It
> doesn't change during localization. Three main characteristics define
> our voice:
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} **Friendly**: A unique and authentic
> voice that is nice to hear. Texts sound pleasant, transparent, and
> honest.
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} **Determined**: This voice sounds
> natural to the ear, clear, and self-confident. Texts are smooth and
> concise.
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} **Willing to help**: A voice you can
> rely on. Texts are easy to understand by everyone (younger and older
> people, subject-matter experts, and the general public), are
> customer-centric (they speak the language of customers), and
> solutions-oriented.
>
> **Tone**
>
> Now it's easier to explain the "tone". The tone can be explained as a
> person's attitude. Depending on the situation, one person can have a
> different attitude. Same here: Depending on the context, a voice can
> have a different tone. However, the voice remains the same. This also
> applies to all languages: There are culture-specific differences in
> each language to bear in mind in order to achieve setting the right
> tone. Our tone can be defined as follows:
>
> **Modern, polite, but casual:** Our tone sounds modern, polite, and
> rather casual (without being too casual: we want to sound
> professional). We prefer a crisp or catchy tone rather than sounding
> flat.
>
> **Natural:** Our texts sound natural, just the way everyone would, in
> a smooth way.
>
> **Straight-forward:** Our tone is straight-forward: the shorter and
> clearer, the better.

# 3 basic principles {#basic-principles .unnumbered}

### Be concise {#be-concise .unnumbered}

> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} Make sure every word you add is
> necessary.
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} Reread your text. If you can make it
> shorter, all the better.

### Easy writing, easy terms {#easy-writing-easy-terms .unnumbered}

> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} Many readers may not be native
> speakers, especially for English. Make sure you use language everybody
> understands. Unless intentional, we want to avoid technical
> terminology and jargon.
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} Make sure that your copy doesn\'t
> have any \'obstacles\' that prevent someone from reading it smoothly
> and fluently. ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} Pay attention to consistency in the
> terminology and sentence styles used throughout.

### Avoid the passive voice {#avoid-the-passive-voice .unnumbered}

> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7226596675415573e-2in"} The active voice helps readers
> understand more quickly. Use the active voice as much as you can.

# Guidelines for English {#guidelines-for-english .unnumbered}

> English is the language of our base copy and the locale used for
> translation and localization into all other languages.
>
> It is always important to remember that users of the English UI will
> be both native speakers and non-native speakers, from the various
> cultural backgrounds. The language must be simple and clear. This
> applies even more to English than to the other languages.
>
> The English variety used is American English.
>
> **Language contents**

### Grammar {#grammar .unnumbered}

> **Spelling**
>
> **Noun endings -sation vs -zation**
>
> See Verb endings -se vs -ze.
>
> **Verb endings -se vs -ze**
>
> We only use spellings with -z- (and not with -s-):

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Correct**                         **Incorrect**
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  customize                           customise

  finalize                            finalise

  organize                            organise
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  analyze                             analyse
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  recognize                           recognise

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

> **Exceptions**: There are only a small set of verbs that must always
> be spelled with -*ise* at the end: advertise, advise, apprise,
> chastise, comprise, compromise, despise, disguise, excise, exercise,
> improvise, incise, prise (i.e. "open"), promise, revise, supervise,
> surmise, surprise, televise.
>
> → Further reading: [English grammar, Oxford
> Dictionaries](https://www.lexico.com/grammar/ize-ise-or-yse)

### Punctuation {#punctuation .unnumbered}

> **Apostrophe**
>
> At DeepL, we use the curly apostrophe:
>
> Correct apostrophe: *...we've been amazed by how quickly\...*
> Incorrect apostrophe: *...we\'ve been amazed by how quickly\...* (as a
> reference, HTML code:
> [*&rsquo;*](https://www.toptal.com/designers/htmlarrows/punctuation/right-single-quotation-mark/))
>
> The use of the straight apostrophe is only intended for displaying
> lines of code or similar tech contents for developers or exceptionally
> if the use of the curly apostrophe is technically impossible (for
> which a solution will be sought for).
>
> **Comma**
>
> At DeepL, we use the Oxford or Serial comma in lists of items. This is
> the comma used after the penultimate item in a list of three or more
> items, before 'and' or 'or':
>
> Correct: *Word (.docx), PowerPoint (.pptx), text (.txt)**,** and .html
> files* Incorrect: *Word (.docx), PowerPoint (.pptx), text (.txt) and
> .html files* Incorrect: *Word (.docx), and PowerPoint (.pptx) files*
>
> → Further reading: [Serial comma, Oxford
> Dictionaries](https://www.lexico.com/definition/serial_comma)
>
> Add a comma after 'e.g.' and 'i.e.':
>
> Correct: DeepL offers translations in many languages (e.g., English,
> Spanish, and German). Incorrect: DeepL offers translations in many
> languages (e.g. English, Spanish, and German).
>
> **En dashes and em dashes**
>
> Use an en dash (--) without spaces on either side. Correct: 2010--2014
>
> Incorrect: 2010 -- 2014
>
> Use an em dash (---) without spaces on either side. Don't substitute
> an en dash for an em dash. Correct: Translate text and protect your
> data---all in one place.
>
> Incorrect: Translate text and protect your data --- all in one place.
>
> Incorrect: Translate text and protect your data -- all in one place.
>
> **Quotation marks**
>
> In American English, both double and single quotation marks are
> accepted. At DeepL, we use the curly double quotation marks:
>
> Correct quotation marks: *Alternately, you can click on
> **"**Customization**"** and enter it manually*. Wrong quotation marks:
> *Alternately, you can click on \"Customization\" and enter it
> manually*.
>
> [HTML code](https://www.toptal.com/designers/htmlarrows/punctuation/)
> -- Opening quotation mark, i.e., **"**,
> [*&ldquo;*](https://www.toptal.com/designers/htmlarrows/punctuation/left-double-quotation-mark/).
> Closing quotation mark, i.e., **"**,
> [*&rdquo;*](https://www.toptal.com/designers/htmlarrows/punctuation/right-double-quotation-mark/).
>
> The use of the straight marks is only intended for displaying lines of
> code or similar tech contents for developers, or exceptionally if the
> use of the curly quotation marks is technically impossible (for which
> a solution will be sought for).
>
> If there is a sentence within the quotation marks that ends in a
> period, the closing quotation mark follows [after]{.underline} the
> period:
>
> Correct: "I find that DeepL not only translates exactly what I want to
> say, but even the tone of voice can be accurate." Incorrect: "I find
> that DeepL not only translates exactly what I want to say, but even
> the tone of voice can be accurate".
>
> **Slashes**
>
> Use a slash (/) without spaces on either side when it divides two
> words. When it divides more than two words, a space can be added on
> either side for clarity. Correct: Choose the tone of your translations
> for languages with formal/informal pronouns.
>
> Correct: You can find more information on the desktop apps / web
> translator here.
>
> Incorrect: Choose the tone of your translations for languages with
> formal / informal pronouns.
>
> **Ellipses**
>
> At DeepL, ellipses (which are most commonly a series of three dots
> with spaces between each) are used to indicate:

### An omission in quoted material

a.  This could be a left-out word, phrase, or line from a quoted source.
    We use ellipses when the omitted text is considered irrelevant or to
    adjust for the grammar surrounding the quoted text. The ellipsis
    must always appear together on the same line.

> Correct: "DeepL's mantra is to embrace competition and . . . to do
> things better." Incorrect: "DeepL's mantra is to embrace competition
> and...to do things better." Incorrect: "DeepL's mantra is to embrace
> competition and. . .to do things better."

b.  However, four dots should be used to indicate the omission of an
    > entire sentence in quoted material.

> Correct: "DeepL's mantra is to embrace competition and use it as a
> motivator to do things better Indeed, for many, DeepL's neural
> network-based translator works better than these
>
> others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have
> missed."
>
> Incorrect**:** "DeepL's mantra is to embrace competition and use it as
> a motivator to do things better Indeed, for many, DeepL's neural
> network-based translator works better than these
>
> others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have
> missed."
>
> Incorrect: "DeepL's mantra is to embrace competition and use it as a
> motivator to do things better Indeed, for many, DeepL's neural
> network-based translator works better than these
>
> others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have
> missed."

c.  Furthermore, ellipses are normally not used before the first word of
    a quotation, even if the beginning of the quoted sentence has been
    left out, or after the last word of a quotation, even if the end of
    the quoted sentence is left out (unless the quote is deliberately
    incomplete, see 2.b.)

> Correct: The mantra at DeepL is "to embrace competition and use it as
> a motivator to do things better." Incorrect: The mantra at DeepL is "
> to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better."

### Faltering speech or an incomplete sentence/thought

d.  This is often seen when quoting spoken word (i.e., interviews or
    podcasts), and it is used for emphasis or additional context, such
    as to show a significant pause.

> Correct: "The next step for DeepL Write is in the works but it's not
> something I can discuss in detail right now."
>
> Incorrect: "The next step for DeepL Write is in the works but it's not
> something I can discuss in detail right now."
>
> Incorrect: "The next step for DeepL Write is in the works. . .but it's
> not something I can discuss in detail right now."

e.  An ellipsis can also be used at the end of a quoted sentence that is
    deliberately left grammatically incomplete.

> Correct: I'd like to draw your attention to the review, which begins
> with "DeepL is by far the best translation app I've used . . ."
> Incorrect: I'd like to draw your attention to the review, which begins
> with "DeepL is by far the best translation app I've used . . .".
> Incorrect: I'd like to draw your attention to the review, which begins
> with "DeepL is by far the best translation app I've used\..."

f.  However, bracketed ellipses can also be used to differentiate your
    own added ellipses (signifying an omission from quoted text) from
    those which exist already to indicate faltering speech of incomplete
    thoughts.

> Correct: "The next step for DeepL Write is in the works. . . but it's
> not something I can discuss in detail right now. \[. . .\] We hope to
> have more detail in the coming months." Incorrect: "The next step for
> DeepL Write is in the works. . . but it's not something I can discuss
> in detail right now. \[\...\] We hope to have more detail in the
> coming months."
>
> Further reading on ellipses: [Ellipses Rules, Chicago Manual of
> Style](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/book/ed17/part2/ch06/psec015.html)

### Usage, style, and conventions {#usage-style-and-conventions .unnumbered}

> **Date format**
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7224409448818897e-2in"} **Full date**. When indicating a
> whole period of time consisting of two full dates, such as a billing
> period, use the following format for dates: *month day year -- month
> day year*, as in *July 14 2020 -- July 13 2021*, or as in *July 14
> 2020 -- August 13 2021*.
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7224409448818897e-2in"} **Full date**. When indicating only
> one date, use the following format for dates: *month day, year,* as in
> *July 31, 2016* (using a comma between the day of the month and the
> year). We use American English: Don\'t use *day month year,* as in *31
> July 2016*. Don't use ordinal numbers in full dates (*July 31st, 2016*
> is incorrect).
>
> ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.7224409448818897e-2in"} If you include the day and the
> month, don't use commas or ordinal numbers: *August 12* (*August 12th*
> is incorrect). ![](media/image2.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} If you include the month and the
> year, don't use commas: *The launch was in June 2020*.
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} If you only include the day, use
> ordinal numbers in full letters, not superscript: *The meeting is on
> the 12th* (*The meeting is on the 12th* is incorrect).
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} For accessibility reasons, the names
> of months should, whenever possible, be spelled out and not
> abbreviated.
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} Slashes in dates: Don't use the form
> *3/5/17*, since non-American users can misunderstand it (some may
> wrongly read the 3rd of May).
>
> **Time format**
>
> **a.m. and p.m. formats:** Use lowercase with periods (e.g., 10 a.m.,
> 10 p.m.). Other forms like uppercase or without periods are also
> acceptable, but lowercase with periods is preferred.
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} **Time of Day**: Use numerals
> followed by \"a.m.\" or \"p.m.\" and separate hours from minutes with
> a colon (e.g., 1:00, 3:30 p.m.).
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} **Omitting :00**: At the top of the
> hour, it\'s acceptable to omit the minutes (e.g., 3 p.m. instead of
> 3:00 p.m.).
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} **Noon and Midnight:** Use the words
> \"noon\" and \"midnight\" rather than 12:00 p.m. and 12:00 a.m. to
> avoid confusion.
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} **Ranges:** For time ranges, use an
> en dash without spaces (e.g., 1:00--3:00 p.m.). Specify \"a.m.\" or
> \"p.m.\" for each time when the range crosses from a.m. to p.m. (e.g.,
> 11:00 a.m.-- 3:00 p.m.).
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} **Avoiding Redundancy:** When a time
> range falls within the morning or afternoon/evening, it\'s not
> necessary to repeat \"a.m.\" or \"p.m.\" if the context is clear
> (e.g., 9--10:30 a.m.), but always prioritize clarity.
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} **Spelling out Time:** Normally spell
> out the time of day in text (e.g., seven o\'clock), but numerals can
> be used for emphasis. Always spell out the number when using
> \"o\'clock\" (e.g., three o\'clock).
>

### When turning spoken word quotes (e.g., from interviews/video or audio recordings) into written/text quotes, we\'re allowed to \"clean up\" the quotes so that they read better in their text version.

> This applies to:
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} subtitles for videos
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722769028871391e-2in"} quotes for content (e.g., blog posts,
> case studies, etc.)
>
> However, there are some exceptions of sources where we **cannot**
> alter the quote in any way: e.g., G2

1.  

### Remove filler words like \"umm\" or \"uh\"

i.  Original quote/Incorrect: "Of course, Europe is a little bit more
    > regulated, **uh**, than the US is, **um**, so there's definitely
    > some more questions that have to be asked about it."

ii. Quote used/Correct: "Of course, Europe is a little bit more
    > regulated than the US is. So, there's definitely more questions
    > that have to be asked about it."

### Fix small grammar errors

iii. Original quote/Incorrect: "So, there **is** differences, there are
     > drawbacks at the beginning . . ."

iv. Quote used/Correct: "So, there **are** differences, there are
    > drawbacks at the beginning . . ."

### Edit for clarity of thought

v.  Original quote/Incorrect: "**Maybe** that's really an advantage to
    > be based out of Europe in that respect for us as lawyers **in that
    > area**.\"

vi. Quote used/Correct: "It's really an advantage to be based out of
    > Europe in that respect for us as lawyers.\"

### Add sentence structure to train-of-thought text

vii. Original quote/Incorrect: "**And** from these corrections, **it
     > is** basically learning all the time, not differently than from
     > what happens when **we are** children and **we are** learning a
     > language, **just** it's modeled in mathematics and executed by a
     > computer."

viii. Quote used/Correct: "From these corrections, **it's** basically
      > learning all the time**---**not differently from what happens
      > when **we're** children, and **we\'re** learning a language.
      > **But**

> it's modeled in mathematics and executed by a computer."

### Remove repeating words

ix. Original quote/Incorrect: "And of course, we need to also process
    > data. And the---kind of **the the the** threshold---we've chose is
    > that we are . . ."

x.  Quote used/Correct: "And of course, we need to also process data.
    > And the---kind of **the** threshold---we've chose is that we are .
    > . ."

### Use ellipses to indicate removals of significant portions of text

xi. Original quote/Incorrect: "**We're talking about AI, the problem
    > with** AI **is that it** relies on the processing of huge amounts
    > of data. And sometimes**, some of the time,** even the most
    > personal data."

xii. Quote used/Correct: "AI relies on the processing of huge amounts of
     > data. And sometimes **. . .** even the most personal data."

### Specifically for subtitles/video captions, add ellipses to indicate:

xiii. Pause between thoughts

      1.  \"This is one of those advantages that I **. . .** which are

      2.  Please see ellipses section for more information

xiv. Sentences that trail off

     1.  "How do you use artificial intelligence and the neural

     2.  Please see ellipses section for more information

### Capitalization {#capitalization .unnumbered}

> **General capitalization**
>
> Use sentence case, which means:
>
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} Capitalize the first word of
> sentences, the first word of each item in a bulleted list, headings,
> CTAs, blog post article titles, SEO page titles, and UI copy
> ![](media/image3.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} Capitalize proper nouns, acronyms,
> and certain brand-related terms specified below
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="2.722769028871391e-2in"
> height="2.722222222222222e-2in"} Use lowercase for everything else
>
> **Proper nouns**
>
> At DeepL, we treat certain words as proper nouns, and therefore they
> need to be capitalized.
>
> This includes: Help Center, Privacy Policy, and Terms & Conditions.
>
> **Branded terms**
>
> Our branded terms, such as product and plan names, are also
> capitalized.
>
> This includes: DeepL Translator, DeepL Pro, Starter, Advanced,
> Ultimate, DeepL API, DeepL API Free, and DeepL API Pro.
>
> **Team names**
>
> Capitalize the names of teams within DeepL but not the word \"team\".
> Correct: Please contact our Sales team.
>
> Incorrect: Please contact our sales team.
>
> **Colon**
>
> Don't capitalize the first word after a colon when it introduces a
> list or is followed by a single sentence. Do capitalize the first word
> after a colon if it's a proper noun. Correct: DeepL Pro offers three
> features for larger companies: team administration, single sign-on
> integration, and advanced data security.
>
> Correct: we offer three features for larger companies: team
> administration, single sign-on integration, and advanced data
> security. Incorrect: We offer three features for larger companies:
> Team administration, single sign-on integration, and advanced data
> security.
>
> **Slashes**
>
> Only capitalize the word after a slash if it's a proper noun or if the
> word before the slash is capitalized. Correct: You can translate more
> documents with an Advanced/Ultimate plan.
>
> Correct: This feature is available for free users / DeepL Pro users.
> Incorrect: This feature is available for DeepL Pro users / Free users.
>
> **Quotation + Capitalization**
>
> At DeepL, when using an incomplete quote from a source where you have
> changed the capitalization to fit into your own text, you must
> indicate the changed capitalisation in brackets. Correct:
> [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/01/11/deepl-the-ai-based-language-translator-raises-over-100m-at-a-1b-valuation/)
> notes DeepL's fortunate position, as "\[a\] lot of startups have been
> struggling to raise rounds" recently.
>
> Incorrect: TechCrunch notes DeepL's fortunate position, as "a lot of
> startups have been struggling to raise rounds" recently.

> **Capitalization**
>
> Use sentence case (capitalize the first word and proper nouns only)
> for headings, subheadings, and buttons.

### Contractions {#contractions .unnumbered}

> Do use contractions to make copy shorter and sound more human.
>
> However, in situations that require a more formal tone (e.g.,
> communication around billing and invoices or legal issues), avoid
> contractions. For example, "can't" can be misread as "can", whereas
> "cannot" ensures the user will understand the negative connotation.

### Numbers {#numbers .unnumbered}

> Use numerals (e.g., 1, 2, 3) instead of writing out numbers, except in
> scenarios where there is a mixed use of numbers. For example, "Press 3
> two times."

### Punctuation {#punctuation-1 .unnumbered}

> **Ampersands**
>
> Avoid using ampersands, unless it's the only option for spacing
> reasons. Ampersands are visually distracting and [cause issues for
> accessibility](https://readabilityguidelines.co.uk/grammar-points/ampersands/).
> Use the word "and" instead.

### Exclamation points {#exclamation-points .unnumbered}

> Use exclamation points sparingly and only in scenarios that require
> extra emphasis. A good rule of thumb is to make sure there is only one
> exclamation mark per page.

> **Research & Citation Guidelines**

### What makes a source qualified/credible?

a.  Generally, there are [five main factors to assess a source's
    credibility](https://www.una.edu/writingcenter/docs/Writing-Resources/Source%20Credibility.pdf):

    i.  **Depth:** "What is the depth of coverage of the information?"
        > Is it only a short overview or something well-researched and
        > detailed?

    ii. **Objectivity:** "Is the information you are using biased in any
        > way? If so, does the bias affect the conclusions of the
        > research? Does the information come from a source that will
        > profit from a particular point of view? If so, the information
        > may not be reliable. Does the source use proper citation?" Are
        > its cited sources also reliable?

    iii. **Currency:** "How up-to-date is the information? When was it
         written?" Generally, more recent data is considered more
         credible---unless you are specifically searching for historical
         data.

    iv. **Authority:** "Who is the author? Does the author have a degree
        in the field? Is the author affiliated with an unbiased
        reputable organization?" Does the author have authority in their
        field/previously published content?

    v.  **Purpose:** "What is the purpose of the source? Is it to
        > entertain, to change public opinion, to present research, or
        > to teach? Who is the intended audience?"

b.  Additionally, another solid indicator of a credible source is
    **peer-review**.

    i.  "Before publication, peer-reviewed journals require that papers
        > be reviewed by experts in the same field. After reading and
        > reviewing the material for accuracy and objectivity, the
        > reviewers will recommend publication, revision, or rejection."

    ii. For these reasons, peer-reviewed sources often serve as highly
        > qualified sources. However, we do not require that sources be
        > peer-reviewed to qualify for use.

### How do we search for a qualified sources?

c.  Google is an acceptable place to begin your search for sources.
    However, it's up to you to determine if the source you've found is
    or isn't credible.

d.  Here are some websites that provide high-quality content/sources:

    i.  [Statista](https://www.statista.com/)

    ii. [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/)

    iii. [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/)

    iv. [Refseek](https://www.refseek.com/)

    v.  [Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/)

e.  Additionally, you can use high-quality outlets as references and
    jumping-off points, such as:

    i.  [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/)

    ii. [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/)

    iii. [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/)

    iv. New York Times

    v.  [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news)

    vi. [Foreign Affairs](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/)

    vii. [Politico](http://www.politico.com/)

    viii. [Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en.html)

### How do we properly reference a source?

f.  As a general rule, we use the [Chicago Manual of
    Style](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/tools_citationguide/citation-guide-1.html#cg-website).
    For blog posts it would make more sense to use endnotes instead of
    in-text citations/links, as this lessens the chances of readers
    clicking away from our content.

g.  As an example:

    i.  **The text:** "As global mobility increases and countries become
        > more diverse, lawyers can expect to serve more multilingual
        > clients and meet more language barriers.1"

    ii. **The citation:** 1. "Increasing Significance of Migration \|
        > Knowledge for Policy." n.d.
        > [Knowledge4policy.ec](http://knowledge4policy.ec/).europa.eu.
        > Accessed April 17, 2023.
        > [https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/increasing-significance-migration_en#:\~:text=Globally%2C%20the%20number%20of%20international](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/increasing-significance-migration_en#%3A~%3Atext%3DGlobally%2C%20the%20number%20of%20international)

h.  You can view sample citations in the Chicago Style
    [here](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/tools_citationguide/citation-guide-1.html#cg-website).

### What needs to be referenced?

i.  **Direct quotes:**

    i.  (Frequency: always) Any direct quotes from outside sources need
        > to be properly cited in an endnote.

        1.  However, quotes from interviews performed by DeepL only need
            > to identify the speaker, their company, and their
            > position.

            a.  **As an example:** According to Dr. Axel Freiherr von
                > dem Bussche, LL.M. (L.S.E.), CIPP/E at Taylor Wessing:
                > "Using AI and technology-enabled tools in the legal
                > sector is becoming increasingly important to remain
                > competitive and improve efficiency."

### Statistics and specific facts:

i.  (Frequency: always) If there are specific numbers/percentages
    > referenced, it needs to be properly cited in an endnote.

    1.  As an example:

        a.  **The text:** According to Gartner research, in-house legal
            > departments will handle 50% of major corporate transaction
            > work using NLP and ML technologies by 20242.

        b.  **The citation:** 2. "5 Legal Technology Trends Changing
            > In-House Legal Departments in 2021." n.d. Gartner.
            > [https://www.gartner.com/smarterwithgartner/5-legal-](https://www.gartner.com/smarterwithgartner/5-legal-technology-trends-changing-in-house-legal-departments)
            > [technology-trends-changing-in-house-legal-departments](https://www.gartner.com/smarterwithgartner/5-legal-technology-trends-changing-in-house-legal-departments).

### Significant claims:

i.  (Frequency: sometimes) In order to support significant claims made,
    > it is helpful to add cited references.

### What sources are not qualified?

j.  Unqualified sources are difficult to name outright but often fall
    into a "you'll know it when you see it" category. To determine if a
    source is not qualified, you should check things like:

    i.  **Domain name:** Many reputable sites end with .edu, .org, or
        > .gov---which can be a good indicator of quality. However, this
        > isn't a fail-proof indicator, so you'll still need to check
        > other factors. Additionally, reputable-looking URLs can be
        > spoofed, so always double-check the domain name.

    ii. **References:** Does the source have references to other
        > websites/sources? If so, do they pass your credibility test?
        > Or is the author providing opinions with no references? If
        > you're on the fence, spend some time verifying the
        > authenticity of the claims your potential source is making.

    iii. **Currency:** Are the sources listed up to date? Are the links
         to sources working, or are the broken/inactive? Sources with
         outdated, non-working source links should not be trusted.

k.  Examples that (most likely) will not count as qualified sources:

    i.  Personal blogs, gossip/entertainment websites, Wikipedia
        > entries, Twitter threads, etc.

    ii. There are always exceptions---for example, if a notable figure
        > makes an important announcement via their Twitter account, you
        > can reference this appropriately.

### What content requires sources/references?

l.  **Blog posts:** Generally, adding sources to a blog post is a good
    idea, as it increases the authority and legitimacy of our articles.
    If a blog post requires sources/references, then they should be
    added as endnotes.

m.  **Social posts:** Most social media posts don't require
    sources/citations. However, if we are quoting someone, it's best to
    tag/mention the author---and if directly referencing a
    statistic/fact, we should mention/tag the source.

n.  **One-pagers:** If we need to add statistics, quotes, or specific
    facts to a one-pager, we should add the reference as a footnote.

o.  **Press releases:** If we need to add statistics, quotes, or
    specific facts to a press release, we should add the reference as an
    endnote.

p.  **Gated content (guides, reports, etc.):** If we need to add
    statistics, quotes, or specific facts to a piece of gated content,
    we should add the reference as a footnote.

q.  **Email campaigns:** If we need to add statistics, quotes, or
    specific facts to an email, we should add the reference as an
    endnote. *(waiting for more feedback from Susi)*

### How do we link out to sources?

r.  In most situations, we'll use footnotes or endnotes to cite our
    sources. If the source includes a link to another website →

    i.  We should use "nofollow" links when possible to preserve our
        > "link juice". *(chatting with Max about this possibility in
        > Contentful)*

    ii. However, if we have a cooperation in place with the source that
        > requires a "follow" link, we can use it.
