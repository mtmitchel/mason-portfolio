Content design at DeepL
At DeepL, content design is organized as a separate craft. Within product development,
content design is represented and embedded through Content Designers (formerly UX
Writers) who are assigned to tracks.
Each Content Designer is responsible for several products and works closely with Product
Managers and Product Designers in every stage of the design process. Ideally, the earlier a
Content Designer is involved in the process the better.
During implementation, Content Designers also often work closely with Localization and
Development to either cover unforeseen cases or ensure that the copy is implemented
according to the original intent and goals.
Due to the synergies and overlaps between products, as well as scaling needs, all Content
Designers at DeepL collaborate daily to come up with and align processes, strategy,
terminology, and standards and to share information and knowledge.
Our mission as the Content Design craft at DeepL
We’re here every day to craft meaningful interface content that enables DeepL users to
easily access and successfully employ DeepL’s powerful Language AI capabilities.
We do this by addressing both individual and industry-specific translation and/or content
creation needs.

General content design principles
Use “you” and “we” for personal pronouns.
Speaking to users (“you”).
Use singular second person (“you”) when speaking to users to make copy more
conversational. Avoid singular first person (“I”) with the exception of legal copy (“I accept the
Terms & Conditions.”).

Speaking as DeepL (“we”)
Use plural first person (“we”) when speaking from DeepL’s point of view. However, overuse
of we, our, and us can make communication feel impersonal or self-centered. Use these
terms sparingly and only when it’s necessary to speak from our perspective, such as in “How
can we help you?”. In most cases, focus on the person you're addressing to make your
message more engaging and user-focused.
Use American English.
We use American English spellings, words, and punctuation. However, we avoid using
culturally-specific idioms or references from the U.S. for two reasons: our English copy is
read by many users who don’t come from the U.S., and it’s difficult to localize
culturally-specific content.
Use sentence case.
For more specific guidelines, see Capitalization.
Front-load important information.
What’s important can be subjective; we try to put information that’s most important to the
user at the beginning.
This often means that we lead with the benefits for the user rather than the features or the
actions. Some examples:
"For unlimited text translation and data security, use your Pro account."
"To get a first draft, select what you want to write"
Be conversational.
Being conversational in content means drawing on the principles of human
conversation—cooperative, pleasant, and natural—to make interactions with our product feel
more human and intuitive. It doesn’t mean informal (human conversations can be formal,
too) or chatty.
Contractions should be used to make copy sound more conversational. For more specific
guidelines, see Contractions.
Link labels should be straightforward, clear, and specific.
Straightforward means that the label should not mislead the user, and set the right
expectations for where the hyperlink will lead them. The more specific a link text is, the
better a user can predict where the link will go.
For example, "learn more" or "find out more" can often be replaced with a more informative
link label about what the user will learn or find out when they visit the link. Good link hygiene
can also help users with screen readers, who may want to jump from link to link to make
sense of the content, especially for long-form copy, such as landing pages.
Use emojis sparingly.

Emojis can often be misinterpreted, so use with caution. Never use emojis to replace words.
Emojis can also make for poor accessibility: not all screen readers may reliably recognize
emojis.
Label your icons.
Icons can help save space, but they can also be ambiguous and slow the user down. While
some icons have a widely-recognized and widely-shared meaning, others are used to mean
different things in different contexts. Whenever possible, icons should be accompanied by a
text label.
Error messages should help the user move forward.
Error messages are for when the user is trying to do something in our product, and it’s not
working. Ideally, these situations wouldn’t occur at all, but when they do, we use error
messages to help them move forward with their goal.
- Make it clear that something is wrong: Be explicit if some action has been
unsuccessful. Then tell the user what they can do about it.
- Practice empathy: Don’t blame the user, and don’t make jokes. Prioritize helping the
user to move on from the problem.
- Prioritize the solution. When appropriate, explain why. Sometimes errors have no
reason. Sometimes the reason isn’t important.
- Try to avoid jargon, such as error codes or technical language that doesn’t provide
the user with useful information. But keep in mind that jargon is subjective
, and
depending on context, it may be more useful to the user to industry-standard terms and
phrases—even if they may sound technical to some audiences.
-
- When it’s our fault, and when the error is serious, do apologize.
Content accessibility (Violet)
Our approach to accessible and inclusive content design at DeepL
Ensuring that our products are accessible to all users is a core tenet of design at DeepL. We
build products to empower communication worldwide, and it's important that these benefits
work for everyone, including people with disabilities.
There are an estimated 253 million people worldwide who have a visual impairment. Users
with a visual impairment often rely on assistive technologies, such as screen readers or
magnifiers. Accessibility can be an issue not just for those with disabilities, but also anyone
in a situation that may restrict their access to a product. For example, someone with a
broken arm, a limited internet connection, or with less literacy or digital literacy can also
benefit from accessibility measures.

To this end, we treat accessibility as an ongoing effort rather than a task to complete. In
practice, this means that we first aim to understand the needs of users with disabilities,
through dedicated user research and ongoing testing. And we keep the following principles
in mind in our product and content design.
1. Use plain language
To ensure that important information is accessible to audiences with intellectual disabilities,
dyslexia, or other reading difficulties, use everyday language and short sentences. Avoid
jargon, long and complicated sentences, and metaphors.
2. Try to avoid directional language, such as “above,” “below,” “left,” or “right.”
Not everyone is reading the page in the same way. Screen reading technologies often
convert the three-dimensional structure of a page into a single line of text, meaning that
items aren't always at the "left” or “right.”
3. In hashtags, use proper capitalization.
4. Opt for labels rather than placeholder text.
5. Structure long-form writing with headers and short paragraphs, to help make the text
skimmable.
6. Write alt text for users, not SEO.
Alt text, or alternative text, is text that describes an image on a web page for screen readers.
Alt text should contain relevant details that communicate the content of an image to the user
of a screen reader. While they should be kept concise—as with a lot of UX copy in general—
do not sacrifice clarity for brevity. Screen readers often process text at a faster speed than
the average reader.
7. Prioritize tool-agnostic language, such as select rather than click or tap.
Not everyone is accessing our website or application with a mouse.
8. Don’t rely on color or visual cues.
Not every user will be perceiving the page visually, which means that colors, for example,
won’t be recognizable to users of screen readers. Icons are best labeled, as they can mean
different things in different contexts, and often aren’t sufficient for communicating what they
are supposed to represent.
For example, don’t rely on red to communicate that an error message is an error message.
9. Ask users
At DeepL, we don’t rely solely on best practices but also use a platform called Fable to
regularly seek feedback on our products from users with disabilities.

10. Keep in mind that rules change
Don’t treat these principles as fixed and eternal, but keep in mind that accessibility
technology and guidelines change all the time.

Voice and tone (Maddie)
Personality
According to the Aaker brand personality model, DeepL is a competent brand.
Why competent?
● Because of our abilities
● Because we’re driven by a desire to be useful
● Because we leave our ego at the door
● Because we’re easy to understand
Voice
DeepL’s voice is static and high-level. It remains consistent across all communications.
There are four characteristics that make up our voice: helpful, empathetic, self-assured, and
professional. These are present in all our brand communications.
Helpful
We’re helpful to our users, providing them with only the necessary information. Keep it brief;
explain yourself clearly. Before you write something, ask yourself “does the user care about
this?” If they do, keep it in. If they don’t, cut it. No matter how complex our technology is, we
explain it quickly and simply, guiding users to their desired goal or intent.
Empathetic
We always put our users first. This means writing more about them than about DeepL (more
“you/your,” less “we/us/our”) and taking the time to analyze and acknowledge the emotional
state that they are in. This also includes using colloquialisms sometimes and getting straight
to the point so the reader is always able to find what they need and care about. It also helps
to acknowledge customer pain points and offer solutions and responses that speak to those.
Self-assured
We build Language AI that benefits people and businesses, so we write with them in mind.
That means always positioning our technology in service of people, rather than falling into
deep scientific descriptions or overly complicated explanations. And it means having the
confidence to own our technological term, Language AI—say it loud, and say it proud.
Professional
We’re in the business of language, and it’s a serious job. Misunderstandings cost businesses
and people countless hours (and money, too). Our users need to trust us, and that’s why we
use a tone that’s courteous and friendly but not overly excited. That said, having a
professional tone doesn’t have to sound cold or clinical.

Tone
The four characteristics that make up DeepL’s voice combine in different ways to create a
tone that suits the context of the communication. Below is a breakdown of tone by situation
for UX content.
Announcements
We use a helpful and professional tone for announcements. Announcements are often
related to new products, plans, features, or releases. Our aim is to provide our users with
only the context and detail they need, getting straight to the point whenever possible. Our
focus is always on how the latest developments can benefit them and their pain points rather
than on what DeepL has accomplished.
Do 🟢Frame the announcement with jobs-to-be-done and user pain points in mind.
Don’t 🔴 Make DeepL the focus of the announcement.
Celebrations
Celebrations are a chance for us to create small moments of accomplishment for our users
through an encouraging and empathetic tone. There are two ends of the spectrum for

celebrations: for small wins, we use a short and sweet approach. For bigger milestones, we
can turn up the excitement, but this should be done sparingly and reserved for situations that
truly deserve their own moment.
Do 🟢Highlight users quick wins like “Nice work! You translated 25 documents this month.”
Don’t 🔴Make small moments bigger than they are like “You translated 500 words. You’re a
superstar!”
Error messages
Errors are a frustrating situation for our users. We use a helpful and empathetic tone. We are
clear and direct, making sure they have a general understanding of what happened and
what they can do to fix it.
Do 🟢Explain the error in the simplest way possible.
Do 🟢Whenever possible, include short instructions on how to fix the error.
Don’t 🔴 Use technical jargon or vague language like “You can’t perform this action.”
Don’t 🔴 Apologize for simple errors like “Sorry, that isn’t a valid email.”
Learning and educating
When educating users, we use a helpful and professional tone. We are often onboarding
them to new products or features or increasing adoption to existing features that they might
not be aware of. Similar to announcements, we want to ensure they can easily find the
information that is relevant to them. This copy should inspire confidence and clarify what
exactly they can gain from the information.
Do 🟢When there’s a lot of information, break things down into digestible chunks.

Don’t 🔴 Overexplain obvious things. We don’t have to spell out every detail for our users,
like which button to click when there is only one primary action.

Grammar and mechanics(Maddie)
Grammar and mechanics
American English
We use American English spellings, words, and punctuation.
American spelling
Keep an eye out for common spelling mistakes like using ‘se’ instead of ‘ze’ or ‘sation’
instead of ‘zation’. When in doubt, check Merriam Webster.
Do 🟢Customize, organization, canceled, behavior
Don’t 🔴Customise, organisation, cancelled, behaviour
Capitalization
Use sentence case, which means:
● Capitalize the first word of sentences, the first word of each item in a bulleted list,
headings, CTAs, SEO page titles, and UI copy
● Capitalize proper nouns, acronyms, and certain brand-related terms
● Use lowercase for everything else
Proper nouns
At DeepL, we treat certain words as proper nouns, and therefore they need to be capitalized.
This includes: Help Center, Privacy Policy, and Terms & Conditions
Branded terms
Our branded terms, such as product and plan names, are also capitalized.
This includes: DeepL Translator, DeepL Pro, Starter, Advanced, Ultimate, DeepL API, DeepL
API Free, and DeepL API Pro
Team names
Capitalize the names of teams but not the word "team".
Do 🟢Please contact our Sales team.
Don’t 🔴Please contact our sales team.
Icon names
Don’t capitalize icon names. Like feature names, icons don’t need to be capitalized as they
are solely used for functional purposes and should not receive unnecessary emphasis.
Do 🟢 Editable entries have a pen icon next to the entry.
Don’t 🔴 Editable entries have a Pen icon next to the entry.
Feature names

Don’t capitalize feature names, as this can be distracting and interrupt the reading flow.
We don’t treat our features as branding tools; they serve a functional purpose only. Branded
terms, such as our product and plan names, are always capitalized. So capitalizing
non-branded feature names would be inconsistent with the risk of diluting our branded terms.
Do 🟢 To apply a different writing style, try DeepL Write Pro for free.
Don’t 🔴 To apply a different Writing Style, try DeepL Write Pro for free.
Exception: when talking about a feature within the UI, we maintain the same capitalization as
seen in the UI to help users recognize it. For example: if we want users to find a button
labeled Glossary, we’d write “Select Glossary" instead of "Select glossary".
Contractions
Do use contractions to shorten copy and sound more human.
However, in situations that require a more formal tone (e.g., communication around billing
and invoices or legal issues), avoid contractions. For example, “can’t” can be misread as
“can”, whereas “cannot” ensures the user will understand the negative connotation.
Punctuation
Ampersands
Avoid using ampersands, unless it’s the only option for spacing reasons. Ampersands are
visually distracting and cause issues for accessibility. Use the word “and” instead.
Commas
Use the Oxford / Serial comma in lists with 3 or more items.
Do 🟢 You can translate Word (.docx), PowerPoint (.pptx), text (.txt), and HTML files.
Don’t 🔴You can translate Word (.docx), PowerPoint (.pptx), text (.txt) and HTML files.
Don’t 🔴 You can translate Word (.docx), and PowerPoint (.pptx) files.
Add a comma after ‘e.g.’ and ‘i.e.’.
Do 🟢 DeepL offers translations in many languages (e.g., English, Spanish, and German).
Don’t 🔴 DeepL offers translations in many languages (e.g. English, Spanish, and German).
Dashes
Hyphen
Use a hyphen (-) for compound terms. If you are unsure if a term needs a hyphen or not,
check Merriam Webster.
Do 🟢 pre-existing
Don’t 🔴 pre–existing

En dash
Use an en dash (–) without spaces on either side for number ranges or compound
adjectives.
Do 🟢 2010–2014
Don’t 🔴 2010 – 2014
Em dash
Use an em dash (—) without spaces on either side. An em dash can be used to substitute
commas, parenthesis, and colons. Don’t substitute an en dash for an em dash.
Do 🟢 Translate text and protect your data—all in one place.
Don’t 🔴 Translate text and protect your data — all in one place.
Don’t 🔴 Translate text and protect your data – all in one place.
Ellipses
Use an ellipsis to indicate that something is in progress. For UX copy, we do not place a
space before an ellipsis or between the periods.
Do 🟢 Translating…
Don’t 🔴 Translating . . .
Exclamation points
Use exclamation points sparingly and only in scenarios that require extra emphasis. A good
rule of thumb is to make sure there is only one exclamation mark per page.
Periods
Avoid using periods in interface copy unless it’s for a full sentence within body copy or
multiple sentences in a row.
Specifically avoid using periods in:
● Headings
● Buttons
● Labels
● Tooltips with only sentence fragments
● Toasts with only sentence fragments
● Bulleted lists with only single sentences or fragments
Quotation marks
Use curly double quotes (“ ”). Don’t use straight double quotes.
Do 🟢 “Our linguists really enjoy working with DeepL-powered translations because of the
superior translation quality.”
Don’t 🔴 "Our linguists really enjoy working with DeepL-powered translations because of the
superior translation quality."

If there is a sentence within the quotation marks that ends in a period, the closing quotation
mark follows after the period:
Do 🟢 “I find that DeepL not only translates exactly what I want to say, but even the tone of
voice can be accurate.”
Don’t 🔴 “I find that DeepL not only translates exactly what I want to say, but even the tone
of voice can be accurate”.

When indicating a period of time, like a billing period, use month day, year – month day, year.
When indicating only one date, use month day, year. Use a comma between the day and the

Don’t 🔴

Localization (Ana)
Writing interface copy for localization
At DeepL, the first version of every piece of copy and content is created in American English.
We also have an in-house Localization team, as well as freelancers (for some of the
languages), who specialize in product copy and more.
Depending on the product, feature, or platform we’re designing for, we localize copy into 18
languages through a dedicated process.
So, do we translate or localize at DeepL? And what’s the difference anyway?
Translation is the process of converting written words from one language into another.
Localization uses different principles to adapt the full meaning of content for another culture
and context. Localization also takes into account layout and formatting, as well as image
adaptation. Translation is only one aspect of localization.
At DeepL, we are indeed committed to localizing content in order to speak to and
support a global audience.
For Content Designers, this means that whenever we iterate, experiment, or add new copy,
we keep localization in mind.
Here are the key principles that guide our work as a Content Design craft when it comes to
localization:
● Be aware of language differences: length, syntax, grammar, measurement and how
it’s expressed, writing systems: left-to-right (e.g., English, Spanish)/right-to-left (e.g.,
Arabic, Hebrew)
Basic example—this is how “Save for later” looks in 9 other languages:
Spanish: Guardar para más tarde
French: Sauvegarder pour plus tard
German: Für später speichern
Italian: Salva per dopo
Portuguese (Brazilian): Salvar para depois

Japanese: 後で保存
Chinese (Simplified): 稍后保存
Russian: Сохранить на потом
Arabic: ظﻔﺣاتﻗوﻠﻟقﺣﻻ
● Respect and embrace cultural differences: the meanings assigned to words, phrases,
and visual elements, as well as seasonality, beliefs, customs, and celebrations
● Place everything in a broader context: is this the right time or way to introduce
something to all of our users?
● Success is a team effort: the success of our copy depends on the success of our
localization, and vice versa. We really are in this together!
Once we've done our part of discovery and initial ideation, and we sit down to actually write,
we also follow a set of industry-recognized best practices for writing with localization in mind.
When writing copy for localization
1. Write in plain English. Not sure how to do that? This might help:
https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/words/use-simple-words-phrases/
https://www.plainenglish.co.uk/how-to-write-in-plain-english.html
2. Keep your copy short and simple (not just microcopy, but also sentences). Avoid
lengthy fillers or unnecessary words.
Do 🟢 Sync data
Don’t 🔴 Initiate the data synchronization process
3. Be consistent in every way: how you use words and terms, capitalization, formatting,
punctuation, voice and tone
4. Use active voice, so language experts can easily identify the subject of the sentence
to further enhance clarity
Do 🟢 We’ve sent you a confirmation email.
Don’t 🔴 A confirmation email was sent to your inbox.
5. Eliminate ambiguity (including homonyms) and replace imprecise pronouns with
actual nouns and use articles before nouns, if the space allows. Spell out acronyms,
especially if you’re introducing something for the first time!
Do 🟢 Select Continue to proceed.
Don’t 🔴 Select the button to continue.
Do 🟢 The glossary generation process will take a few minutes.

Don’t 🔴 It will take a few minutes.
6. Avoid using jargon, slang, and potentially untranslatable phrases or idioms
Do 🟢 Easily deploy and manage your applications on our powerful cloud platform.
Don’t 🔴 Leverage our robust, cloud-native infrastructure to seamlessly provision
and orchestrate your mission-critical workloads.
Do 🟢 Our technology is superior to other options on the market.
Don’t 🔴 Our technology is a cut above the rest.
7. Don’t use puns or humor—probably impossible to reproduce in other languages with
the same effect and potentially disrespectful
Do 🟢 Your account is ready. Let's start!
Don’t 🔴 You're all set! Let's get this show on the road.
8. When highlighting parts of your copy, be aware of potential syntax differences
9. Don’t split sentences on different UI elements because it’s hard to follow for any
language expert (or user!) and due to… again, syntax differences, it might actually be
impossible to reproduce the split in other languages
10. Avoid using examples or references that may not make sense in other cultural
contexts, like historical or seasonal events
11. If you’re unsure about context or cultural differences, consult the Localization team
before making the final copy decision
12. Avoid putting text on images whenever possible. Reasons? The length of the text
breaks up the design, adds overhead, and requires additional manual design work.
13. Work with your designers to make sure the design will scale well when localizing. Not
sure of all the technical details? Ask!
When handing off the copy
1. Add relevant context for the Localization team whenever possible (not just visually)
2. If you're introducing a new key term or a new product or feature name, be sure to
specify it when you hand off the copy so that it can be properly documented and
consistently applied from that point on
3. Advocate for localization testing by QA
What is localization testing?

Localization testing, as a broader concept, looks at how well the localization process has
succeeded in adapting the product to a specific region, locale, or culture.
For example, when testing the localization of a feature, QA might focus on checking (some
of) the following:
● Whether all text is translated and rendered correctly
● Whether all media contains properly localized content
● If there are any inconsistencies in date formatting
● If numbers or currencies are formatted correctly
This is just one last step before the actual go-live to ensure that shared success we
mentioned earlier.
