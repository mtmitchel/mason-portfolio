Content style guide
Content style guide (for English)
About this style guide
At DeepL, we are on a mission to overcome language barriers and bring cultures closer together. As a language technology company, we aim to display clear and seamless copy
wherever users and customers interact with our product and business. Our UI is one of our most powerful levers to show that we care about language. The purpose of this style guide is
to offer a set of language rules in order to:
Achieve consistency across all company copy regardless of the copywriter
Save time for copywriting and translating tasks
Make sure that our copy and translations have the quality we expect to provide the best user experience
The needs and the contents of our style guide will evolve, so it will be revisited periodically to include new elements as new questions come up.

Guidelines for English
English is the language of our base copy and the locale used for translation and localization into all other languages.
It is always important to remember that users of the English UI will be both native speakers and non-native speakers, from the various cultural backgrounds. The language must be
simple and clear. This applies even more to English than to the other languages.
The English variety used is American English.

Ellipses
At DeepL, ellipses (which are most commonly a series of three dots with spaces between each) are used to indicate:
1. An omission in quoted material
a. This could be a left-out word, phrase, or line from a quoted source. We use ellipses when the omitted text is considered irrelevant or to adjust for the grammar surrounding the
quoted text. The ellipsis must always appear together on the same line.
Correct: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and . . . to do things better.”
Incorrect: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and…to do things better.”
Incorrect: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and. . .to do things better.”
b. However, four dots should be used to indicate the omission of an entire sentence in quoted material.
Correct: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better. . . . Indeed, for many, DeepL’s neural network-based translator works better than these
others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have missed.”
Incorrect: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better. . . Indeed, for many, DeepL’s neural network-based translator works better than these
others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have missed.”
Incorrect: “DeepL’s mantra is to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better. . .Indeed, for many, DeepL’s neural network-based translator works better than these
others, capturing certain nuances and meanings that rivals have missed.”
c. Furthermore, ellipses are normally not used before the first word of a quotation, even if the beginning of the quoted sentence has been left out, or after the last word of a
quotation, even if the end of the quoted sentence is left out (unless the quote is deliberately incomplete, see 2.b.)
Correct: The mantra at DeepL is “to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better.”
Incorrect: The mantra at DeepL is “. . . to embrace competition and use it as a motivator to do things better.”
2. Faltering speech or an incomplete sentence/thought
a. This is often seen when quoting spoken word (i.e., interviews or podcasts), and it is used for emphasis or additional context, such as to show a significant pause.
Correct: “The next step for DeepL Write is in the works . . . but it’s not something I can discuss in detail right now.”
Incorrect: “The next step for DeepL Write is in the works... but it’s not something I can discuss in detail right now.”
Incorrect: “The next step for DeepL Write is in the works. . .but it’s not something I can discuss in detail right now.”
b. An ellipsis can also be used at the end of a quoted sentence that is deliberately left grammatically incomplete.
Correct: I’d like to draw your attention to the review, which begins with “DeepL is by far the best translation app I’ve used . . .”
Incorrect: I’d like to draw your attention to the review, which begins with “DeepL is by far the best translation app I’ve used . . .”.

Incorrect: I’d like to draw your attention to the review, which begins with “DeepL is by far the best translation app I’ve used...”
c. However, bracketed ellipses can also be used to differentiate your own added ellipses (signifying an omission from quoted text) from those which exist already to indicate faltering
speech of incomplete thoughts.
Correct: “The next step for DeepL Write is in the works. . . but it’s not something I can discuss in detail right now. [. . .] We hope to have more detail in the coming months.”
Incorrect: “The next step for DeepL Write is in the works. . . but it’s not something I can discuss in detail right now. [...] We hope to have more detail in the coming months.”
Further reading on ellipses: Ellipses Rules, Chicago Manual of Style
Usage, style, and conventions
Date format
Full date. When indicating a whole period of time consisting of two full dates, such as a billing period, use the following format for dates: month day year – month day year, as in July
14 2020 – July 13 2021, or as in July 14 2020 – August 13 2021.
Full date. When indicating only one date, use the following format for dates: month day, year, as in July 31, 2016 (using a comma between the day of the month and the year). We
use American English: Don't use day month year, as in 31 July 2016. Don’t use ordinal numbers in full dates (July 31st, 2016 is incorrect).
If you include the day and the month, don’t use commas or ordinal numbers: August 12 (August 12th is incorrect).
If you include the month and the year, don’t use commas: The launch was in June 2020.
If you only include the day, use ordinal numbers in full letters, not superscript: The meeting is on the 12th (
The meeting is on the 12
th 
is incorrect).
For accessibility reasons, the names of months should, whenever possible, be spelled out and not abbreviated.
Slashes in dates: Don’t use the form 3/5/17, since non-American users can misunderstand it (some may wrongly read the 3rd of May).
Time format
a.m. and p.m. formats: Use lowercase with periods (e.g., 10 a.m., 10 p.m.). Other forms like uppercase or without periods are also acceptable, but lowercase with periods is
preferred.
Time of Day: Use numerals followed by "a.m." or "p.m." and separate hours from minutes with a colon (e.g., 1:00, 3:30 p.m.).
Omitting :00: At the top of the hour, it's acceptable to omit the minutes (e.g., 3 p.m. instead of 3:00 p.m.).
Noon and Midnight: Use the words "noon" and "midnight" rather than 12:00 p.m. and 12:00 a.m. to avoid confusion.
Ranges: For time ranges, use an en dash without spaces (e.g., 1:00–3:00 p.m.). Specify "a.m." or "p.m." for each time when the range crosses from a.m. to p.m. (e.g., 11:00 a.m.–
3:00 p.m.).
Avoiding Redundancy: When a time range falls within the morning or afternoon/evening, it's not necessary to repeat "a.m." or "p.m." if the context is clear (e.g., 9–10:30 a.m.), but
always prioritize clarity.
Spelling out Time: Normally spell out the time of day in text (e.g., seven o'clock), but numerals can be used for emphasis. Always spell out the number when using "o'clock" (e.g.,
three o'clock).

Proper nouns
At DeepL, we treat certain words as proper nouns, and therefore they need to be capitalized.
This includes: Help Center, Privacy Policy, and Terms & Conditions.
Branded terms
Our branded terms, such as product and plan names, are also capitalized.
This includes: DeepL Translator, DeepL Pro, Starter, Advanced, Ultimate, DeepL API, DeepL API Free, and DeepL API Pro.
Team names
Capitalize the names of teams but not the word "team".
Correct: Please contact our Sales team.
Incorrect: Please contact our sales team.

Colon
Don’t capitalize the first word after a colon when it introduces a list or is followed by a single sentence. Do capitalize the first word after a colon if it’s a proper noun.
Correct: DeepL Pro offers three features for larger companies: team administration, single sign-on integration, and advanced data security.
Correct: we offer three features for larger companies: team administration, single sign-on integration, and advanced data security.
Incorrect: We offer three features for larger companies: Team administration, single sign-on integration, and advanced data security.

Only capitalize the word after a slash if it’s a proper noun or if the word before the slash is capitalized.

Research & Citation Guidelines
1. What makes a source qualified/credible?
a. Generally, there are five main factors to assess a source’s credibility:
i. Depth: “What is the depth of coverage of the information?” Is it only a short overview or something well-researched and detailed?
ii. Objectivity: “Is the information you are using biased in any way? If so, does the bias affect the conclusions of the research? Does the information come from a source that will
profit from a particular point of view? If so, the information may not be reliable. Does the source use proper citation?” Are its cited sources also reliable?
iii. Currency: “How up-to-date is the information? When was it written?” Generally, more recent data is considered more credible—unless you are specifically searching for
historical data.
iv. Authority: “Who is the author? Does the author have a degree in the field? Is the author affiliated with an unbiased reputable organization?” Does the author have authority in
their field/previously published content?
v. Purpose: “What is the purpose of the source? Is it to entertain, to change public opinion, to present research, or to teach? Who is the intended audience?”
b. Additionally, another solid indicator of a credible source is peer-review.
i. “Before publication, peer-reviewed journals require that papers be reviewed by experts in the same field. After reading and reviewing the material for accuracy and objectivity,
the reviewers will recommend publication, revision, or rejection.”
ii. For these reasons, peer-reviewed sources often serve as highly qualified sources. However, we do not require that sources be peer-reviewed to qualify for use.

5. What sources are not qualified?
a. Unqualified sources are difficult to name outright but often fall into a “you’ll know it when you see it” category. To determine if a source is not qualified, you should check things
like:
i. Domain name: Many reputable sites end with .edu, .org, or .gov—which can be a good indicator of quality. However, this isn’t a fail-proof indicator, so you’ll still need to check
other factors. Additionally, reputable-looking URLs can be spoofed, so always double-check the domain name.
ii. References: Does the source have references to other websites/sources? If so, do they pass your credibility test? Or is the author providing opinions with no references? If
you’re on the fence, spend some time verifying the authenticity of the claims your potential source is making.
iii. Currency: Are the sources listed up to date? Are the links to sources working, or are the broken/inactive? Sources with outdated, non-working source links should not be
trusted.
b. Examples that (most likely) will not count as qualified sources:
i. Personal blogs, gossip/entertainment websites, Wikipedia entries, Twitter threads, etc.

ii. There are always exceptions—for example, if a notable figure makes an important announcement via their Twitter account, you can reference this appropriately.
6. What content requires sources/references?
a. Blog posts: Generally, adding sources to a blog post is a good idea, as it increases the authority and legitimacy of our articles. If a blog post requires sources/references, then
they should be added as endnotes.
b. Social posts: Most social media posts don’t require sources/citations. However, if we are quoting someone, it’s best to tag/mention the author—and if directly referencing a
statistic/fact, we should mention/tag the source.
c. One-pagers: If we need to add statistics, quotes, or specific facts to a one-pager, we should add the reference as a footnote.
d. Press releases: If we need to add statistics, quotes, or specific facts to a press release, we should add the reference as an endnote.
e. Gated content (guides, reports, etc.): If we need to add statistics, quotes, or specific facts to a piece of gated content, we should add the reference as a footnote.
f. Email campaigns: If we need to add statistics, quotes, or specific facts to an email, we should add the reference as an endnote. (waiting for more feedback from Susi)
7. How do we link out to sources?
a. In most situations, we’ll use footnotes or endnotes to cite our sources. If the source includes a link to another website →
i. We should use “nofollow” links when possible to preserve our “link juice”. (chatting with Max about this possibility in Contentful)
ii. However, if we have a cooperation in place with the source that requires a “follow” link, we can use it.
