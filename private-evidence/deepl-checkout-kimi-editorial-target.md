## Increasing DeepL Pro conversions by rethinking the checkout story

### Context and challenge

As part of the monetization team, I was asked to help improve conversion rates
for DeepL Pro subscriptions by reworking the checkout experience. The existing
flow was a three-step journey—Account, Details, Review—that behaved more like a
bureaucratic form than a product story guiding people into a high-value trial.
It was functional, but it asked users to commit to a paid subscription while
holding back the reassurance, clarity, and momentum that make commitment feel
safe.

The business goal was clear: increase the number of paid monthly and annual
subscriptions. My challenge as UX copywriter and content designer was to
translate that commercial goal into a user-centred narrative across the
checkout, and to do it in a way we could test and measure.

Across monthly and annual subscriptions, the experiment we shipped increased
paid subscriptions by 3.02% and generated approximately €2.4 million in
incremental annual recurring revenue.

### Partnering with product to reshape the flow

I started by mapping the existing checkout journey with the product manager,
paying attention to where copy forced users to “work” instead of helping them
decide. Together, we identified three main issues:

- The three steps fragmented the decision: users set up an account, filled in
  billing details, and only then saw a full summary and final CTA. The value of
  the trial and plan never came together in a single, coherent story.
- The trial proposition (“free cancellation within 30 days”) was technically
  correct, but weak as a trust signal. It felt legalistic instead of
  reassuring.
- Key moments of anxiety—“When will I be charged?”, “Can I cancel?”, “What
  exactly am I getting?”—were answered late and in dense text.

Our hypothesis was simple: if we reduce cognitive load, surface reassurance
earlier, and make the value of the plan and trial explicit at the moment of
payment, more users will feel comfortable completing checkout.

To test this, we worked towards a two-step flow that still respected technical
constraints but changed what each step *said* and *signaled*: Account, then a
combined Details + Review step.

### Strategy: treating checkout as a narrative, not a form

My approach was guided by a few core principles:

- **Cognitive load and flow**: Fewer steps don’t just mean fewer clicks; they
  mean fewer chances to lose focus. I aimed to reduce mental overhead by
  consolidating information and stripping out redundant explanations.
- **Information hierarchy**: Users should see what matters most at the exact
  moment it matters. For checkout, that meant: trial conditions, price, “due
  today,” and cancellation policy at the moment they reach for their card.
- **Trust and risk reduction**: Checkout is an exercise in risk perception.
  Clear language around free trial, “€0 due today,” and cancellation windows
  needed to be visible and scannable, not buried in legal copy.
- **Choice architecture**: The way plans and billing options are framed can
  nudge users toward higher-value choices without feeling manipulative. Annual
  vs. monthly, savings, and feature highlights needed to be obvious but honest.
- **Consistency and tone**: DeepL’s brand voice is confident but not pushy. The
  copy needed to feel like guidance from a helpful expert, not hard-sell
  marketing.

### Execution: from three steps to one coherent decision moment

In the new experiment, I focused on a few key changes in how the flow “talked”
to users:

1. **Reframing the trial at the point of payment**  
   Instead of a generic “free cancellation within 30 days,” I moved to language
   that makes the risk profile explicit and tangible: emphasizing the 30-day
   trial, “no payment until end of trial,” and “cancel anytime” near the primary
   button. This turns an abstract policy into concrete user benefits. Placing
   these messages directly under the “Begin subscription” CTA gives users
   immediate answers to “What happens if I change my mind?” before they commit.

2. **Clarifying what’s due today vs. later**  
   The legacy flow showed the annual price and a generic total. In the rebuilt
   version, “Due today: €0” and “Total price after trial” are clearly separated.
   This is a deliberate use of information hierarchy: the risk-reducing message
   (“you pay nothing now”) sits at the top, while the commitment (“€299.88 per
   year after the trial”) follows. Users see both, but their first impression is
   one of safety.

3. **Bringing features into the checkout, not just the marketing site**  
   Previously, the Review step repeated plan details but in a long, text-heavy
   block. I restructured this into a concise feature list in the sidebar (e.g.,
   maximum data security, limits, glossaries, usage in CAT tools), so users can
   check “Is this worth it?” while filling in billing details instead of only
   after. This uses the principle of progressive disclosure: enough detail to
   feel informed, but not so much that it overwhelms.

4. **Reducing step friction without hiding important information**  
   Moving from three steps to two could easily become a dark pattern if users
   lost access to key information. To avoid that, I combined the “Details” and
   “Review” content in one layout: billing + payment fields on the left,
   summary, trial conditions, and plan features on the right. The copy and
   layout work together: users never lose sight of what they’re signing up for
   while they type.

5. **Sharpening microcopy for clarity and confidence**  
   Small changes—like renaming the primary CTA to “Begin subscription” instead
   of a generic “Buy now,” and aligning error/help text with the overall
   tone—reinforce the sense that the user is starting a relationship, not just
   completing a transaction. I treated every label, hint, and footer note as
   part of the narrative, not as filler.

Throughout the process, I iterated closely with the PM and design partner. We
reviewed event data and funnel drop-offs to ensure we weren’t just making the
flow “simpler,” but actually addressing known friction points. The experiment
was scoped to allow an A/B test, so we could tie copy and flow changes to
measurable impact rather than subjective preference.

### Outcome: measurable revenue, plus a reusable pattern

Once launched, the two-step checkout experiment delivered a 3.02% uplift in
paid subscriptions across monthly and annual plans and drove approximately €2.4
million in incremental annual recurring revenue. That’s a material business
impact from what could easily have been perceived as “just some words on a
page.”

Equally important, the test validated a broader principle for the team:
treating checkout copy as a strategic product surface—one that shapes perceived
risk, trust, and value—is as important as visual design or pricing strategy.
The patterns we developed here (clear “due today” messaging, visible trial
reassurance, integrated feature storytelling) became reference points for
future experiments in onboarding and upsell flows.

### Conclusion: why this matters beyond one experiment

For me, this project underscored that UX copy and content design are not the
last layer of polish; they are levers of behavior and revenue. When the text and
structure of a checkout flow lean into user psychology—reducing cognitive load,
clarifying risk, and articulating value at the moment of decision—conversion
becomes the outcome of *understanding*, not pressure.

This case isn’t just about moving from three steps to two or about hitting a
conversion target. It’s about reframing the role of language in product: from
static labels to dynamic, evidence-based design decisions. The next time a team
looks at a “simple” form, the question isn’t just “How can we make this
shorter?” but “What story is this form telling—and is that story helping users
make the decision we’re asking of them?”
