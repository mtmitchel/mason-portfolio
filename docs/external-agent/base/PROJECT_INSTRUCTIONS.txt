WRITING STANDARD

- Use plain English, concrete verbs, and clear agency. Explain decisions and
  tradeoffs; credit collaborators without understating Mason's contribution.
- Let the introduction introduce. Orient the reader to the company or product,
  situation, pressure or opportunity, and why the work began. Do not summarize
  every decision, artifact, and result before the story develops. Front-load
  the useful point within each paragraph or section, not the whole case into
  its first screen.
- Build transitions from a changed condition, decision, consequence, contrast,
  or unresolved question that makes the next section necessary. Generic time
  markers and headings alone do not create narrative movement.
- Make titles and headings specific. Orient first-time readers. Explain only
  unfamiliar terms needed to follow the argument, then keep each term stable.
- Keep one term per concept. Exact repetition is clearer than renaming unless a
  nearby echo adds nothing. Cut or recast before substituting, and preserve
  meaning, emphasis, supported detail, and structure.
- After structural revision, line-edit and proofread the entire case. Correct
  accidental fragments, run-ons, comma splices, faulty parallelism, unclear
  pronouns, and punctuation errors. Use punctuation by grammatical function,
  avoid repeated em dashes, and read the final copy aloud.
- Give the case a deliberate conclusion. Synthesize rather than recap; return
  to the opening pressure or question, interpret Mason's contribution, and end
  on a concrete case-specific result, consequence, product change, or judgment.
  Do not trail off into a generic lesson, future work, caveat, or reusable line.
- Cut jargon, inflated stakes, synthetic polish, generic phrases, screen tours,
  inventories, and chronology posing as story.
