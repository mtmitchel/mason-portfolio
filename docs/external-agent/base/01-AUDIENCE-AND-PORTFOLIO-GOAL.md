# Audience and portfolio goal

Mason Mitchel is a UX copywriter and content designer whose work spans product,
growth, subscriptions, pricing, enterprise accounts, and product marketing.

## The portfolio goal

When Mason explicitly asks to evaluate, select, or rebuild the overall portfolio
slate, build the strongest overall site: roughly five convincing cases selected
across Mason's DeepL experience and the full available evidence base. Do not
assume that existing case pages, old case names, prior groupings, or current
screens are the right portfolio. For any focused request—including a case,
passage, page, card, homepage, navigation element, or site component—use this
goal as context without reopening the full slate.

Look for combinations that connect:

- a documented company or team goal, commercial priority, customer need, or
  meaningful product pressure;
- Mason's role, collaborators, decisions, and judgment;
- existing Figma visuals or other concrete artifacts that make the work
  understandable; and
- an attributable data-driven result at the correct team, program, experiment,
  or campaign level when one is supported.

Not every strong case needs all four. These are relationships to explore, not
mandatory public sections or a scoring formula. Related initiatives may form
one stronger story when their causal connection is real. Unrelated work should
not be bundled merely to make a case larger.

Judge the slate in totality. Each selected case should add a distinct hiring
signal instead of repeating the same result, before-and-after construction, or
visual grammar.

## The readers

The primary readers are:

- recruiters screening quickly for relevant experience;
- content-design and UX-writing hiring managers; and
- product, design, and growth leaders evaluating judgment and collaboration.

They do not need an exhaustive record of every screen Mason touched. They need
a selective account that makes his value easy to understand and easy to
remember.

A strong case should leave them able to say:

- what product, customer, or organizational problem mattered;
- what made the work difficult or consequential;
- what Mason personally wrote, changed, decided, challenged, or made possible;
- how the chosen evidence supports that account;
- what changed or what the work demonstrates; and
- why that capability would help their own product or organization.

Organizational value is broader than direct revenue attribution. Depending on
the case, useful work can help people:

- understand and buy the right offer;
- complete a difficult or high-stakes task;
- adopt and keep using a product;
- deploy a product across a company;
- trust a product with sensitive or consequential work;
- recover from failure without abandoning the task; or
- work consistently across products, teams, languages, and changing plans.

## The site as a hiring artifact

The homepage and project index are part of the argument. On the first screen, a
reader should understand Mason's role, the useful focus of his work, and where
to find proof. Each project card should make sense without the click: name the
product or problem, the meaningful work or change, and one supported signal of
impact or relevance. Do not compress the whole case into the card.

The portfolio should be memorable because the work, judgment, and evidence are
concrete—not because the prose exaggerates their importance or every page uses
the same construction.

## Business value is part of the hiring signal

Companies hire portfolios to judge whether a candidate can help a product or
organization create value. When a case has a documented commercial goal or
measured business outcome, make that context visible early and connect Mason's
work to it without claiming unsupported causation. Do not reduce the case to a
metric: explain the customer or product change that made the outcome relevant.

When no direct revenue result exists, state the strongest supported value at
the correct scope—such as conversion, adoption, retention, task completion,
trust, reduced support burden, or operational scale. Never invent a business
impact to make a case sound more commercial.
