# Mason writing voice and sample notes

This is Mason's persistent Portfolio voice reference. It supports both
evaluation and authorized drafting, rewriting, and line editing. It complements
the story guidance in 02. It does not govern facts, claims, terminology, case
structure, conclusions, or authorization.

## Precedence and limits

- The current task, supplied evidence, Mason's corrections, and any applicable
  brand or publication guide outrank these samples.
- Use the samples for cadence, directness, warmth, explanation density,
  transitions, and reader relationship only. Never import their facts,
  examples, metrics, claims, headings, metaphors, structure, conclusions, or
  rhetorical habits into another case.
- Match the sample to the genre. Do not average every sample into one generic
  voice or reproduce a passage's structure sentence by sentence.
- Treat public pages as selected examples of Mason's writing range, not proof
  of sole authorship, untouched final wording, or current factual accuracy.
  Brand editing may be present.
- Do not use this editorial voice as the default for product-interface copy,
  CV bullets, technical documentation, email, or another person's writing.

## Selected corpus

Mason selected the SmartRecruiters and Localyze sections of his public writing
portfolio for this reference. Airy, Joblift, Kitchen Stories, and gallereplay
work is outside the selected corpus.

The corpus contains several writing situations. SmartRecruiters articles and
guides use a company or publication voice. The Hiring Success interviews mix
edited introductions with spoken questions and transcripts. Localyze articles
use a B2B brand voice, and the Forbes council posts were written on behalf of
Localyze's CEO. Those contexts demonstrate range; none is a universal personal
voice.

## Durable voice traits

- **Direct and grounded.** State the real pressure or practical concern in
  concrete language without inflating it.
- **Clear with complexity.** Define unfamiliar terms, provide enough context,
  and use plain transitions so a reader can follow a dense subject.
- **Close to the reader.** Use direct address or contractions when they make
  the meaning clearer and fit the route and audience.
- **Natural in its turns.** Use supported contrast and consequence without
  canned pivots, theatrical reveals, or inflated signposting.
- **Varied in rhythm.** Let longer explanatory sentences carry context and use
  shorter sentences for emphasis. Do not make every paragraph sound alike.
- **Informed but human.** Prefer concrete nouns, confident explanation, warmth,
  and restraint over institutional distance.

## Annotated voice cues

These short excerpts are memory cues, not copy templates. Open a linked full
piece only when Mason explicitly requests outside research in the current chat
or supplies the full piece as a source in that chat.

### Complicate a familiar idea

From [The Problem With Culture Fit and What Can Be Done About It](https://www.smartrecruiters.com/resources/article/the-problem-with-culture-fit-and-what-can-be-done-about-it/):

> Put them together, however, and things change a bit.

This excerpt illustrates a measured pivot that complicates a familiar
assumption without theatrical framing.

### Connect commercial stakes to a concrete case

From [How SmartRecruiters Could Save You a Million Dollars](https://www.smartrecruiters.com/resources/article/how-smartrecruiters-could-save-you-a-million-dollars/):

> For some this may be inconvenient; for others it may be a blessing in disguise.

This excerpt illustrates business-facing prose that sounds confident,
concrete, and restrained rather than promotional.

### Explain through a human analogy

From [Everything You Need to Know About Onboarding](https://www.smartrecruiters.com/resources/article/everything-you-need-to-know-about-onboarding-in-2020/):

> Think of onboarding as an extended handshake and hello.

This older editorial example illustrates how analogy can make an explanation
easy to follow; its facts and assumptions do not transfer to another task.

### Bring curiosity into an interview or profile

From [Discussions on Hiring as a Global Humanism](https://hiringsuccess.com/episode-10-discussions-on-hiring-as-a-global-humanism/):

> The discussion that followed is one that you're sure to appreciate.

The edited introduction and Mason's questions illustrate concise question
framing and responsive follow-ups. Its conversational register, transcript
fillers, and spoken fragments do not transfer to case-study prose.

### Move from external change to product response

From [Localyze 2.0: The next generation of global mobility tech](https://www.localyze.com/blog/meet-localyze-2-0-the-next-generation-of-global-mobility-technology):

> This isn't your run-of-the-mill product release, though.

This excerpt illustrates movement from business context to concrete feature
detail. Its idiom and promotional energy do not transfer to case-study prose.

### Turn research into an argument

From [Hiring Overseas Talent to Tackle the UK Labour Shortage](https://media.journoportfolio.com/users/48175/uploads/49f5b471-71df-46ad-b1ad-21cd43793912.pdf):

> The primary purpose of this report, though, is not to outline the merits of international hiring.

This report illustrates evidence-led prose with explicit questions, restrained
claims, and readable transitions between findings and interpretation. It serves
a UK audience and uses British English; that spelling does not transfer to
current Portfolio copy.

## What not to imitate

The samples span 2019–2022 work, client voices, publication conventions, and
UK- and US-facing assignments. Preserve their strengths without copying:

- unsupported promotional claims, superlatives, or causal certainty;
- dated facts, statistics, product descriptions, or terminology;
- rhetorical habits that obscure the supported actor, action, condition, or
  consequence;
- repeated transition words such as `undoubtedly`, `evidently`, or `ultimately`;
- legacy grammar, punctuation, capitalization, or spelling choices;
- the CEO persona of the Forbes posts;
- spoken fillers and transcript errors; or
- a source's outline, heading pattern, or conclusion as a reusable template.

Current Mason corrections outrank every sample. If he rejects a draft as not
sounding like him, reconsider the voice choices instead of defending a
mechanically compliant imitation.
